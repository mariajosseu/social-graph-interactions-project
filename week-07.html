Modern language models turn the same Marvel pages into a new kind of map. This week asks what those vectors capture, what they leave out, and which questions become possible once text has a geometry.

What does the graph miss when the text starts talking?

What I am asking. Use the Marvel pages and the Week 01 network to find one disagreement between semantic similarity and hyperlink structure. The point is not to throw five models at the corpus. It is to choose one question, make one convincing figure, and read the pages underneath the result.

Working question: which characters are close in meaning but far apart in the network, and what does their text reveal about the difference?

Question 02 / The instrumentDOCUMENT EMBEDDING × COSINE

Figure 02 / The same pages, represented differently

First decide what a dot means.

A pretrained encoder gives each page a dense vector. Long pages are split into token-limited chunks, each chunk is embedded, and the chunk vectors are mean-pooled into one representation per page. Cosine similarity is calculated in that original space; a 2D map is only a drawing of it.

Question 03 / The picturePCA × T-SNE

Figure 03 / A map is an argument

Can a picture disagree with the measurement?

The atlas will show the same document vectors through two projections. PCA gives a broad linear view; t-SNE emphasizes local neighborhoods. The coordinates can move when the projection changes, while the original vectors and their nearest-neighbor rankings stay fixed.

Textual check

The strongest pair will be inspected in the actual pages. Are they close because they share a setting, a publishing history, a power, or just the same generic Wikipedia language?

Limitation. An embedding captures patterns learned from other text, not a ground-truth measure of fictional relatedness. A missing hyperlink is not evidence that two characters are unrelated, and a nearby dot is not proof that two pages mean the same thing.
